Will the last bus run on Monday?

Leena usually takes the 8:40 bus home from rehearsal. On Friday afternoon, someone shares a timetable in a parents’ group, and her bus is missing. By the time a reader forwards it to the West Ward Chronicle, people are already saying the service will end on Monday.

In the newsroom

You’re editing the Chronicle’s local news bulletin. It goes out at five, and your readers want to know whether they can rely on the bus after rehearsal.

Where to begin

A colleague has already spoken to Leena. Her rehearsal finishes at eight, twenty minutes after the earlier bus leaves the Arts Centre. She can explain what losing the later service would mean for her. To find out whether it is actually being withdrawn, you’ll need to trace the screenshot to the document it came from.

You have the original document, the current timetable and your colleague’s interview notes. Use them to draft a headline for your editor while you wait to hear from the operator. You can pause and come back at any point.

Studying media and communication

Part of studying media and communication is following a story as it passes from one person to another. Here, the person who wrote the timetable, the parent who forwarded it and the reader who sent it to the paper may each understand the same row of departure times differently. Comparing what each person saw will help you explain how the story began.

Once you have the facts, you still have an editorial choice to make. Leena needs to plan her journey, while other residents may want a say in the future of the service. You’ll decide which reader to address in the opening line of your bulletin.

Where did the timetable come from?

Start with the forwarded timetable and open the original beside it. Compare the departure times, then look just above them. Does the caption in the parents’ group still seem accurate?
