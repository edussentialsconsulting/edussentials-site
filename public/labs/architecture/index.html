The librarian’s brief

“On paper we have enough seats. Come at four and you’ll see why the room doesn’t work.”

What architects do

An architect asks how a building will be inhabited. In a small library that means considering who arrives together, where light falls, how a reader finds a seat and what happens when someone stops to talk. A measured drawing makes certain claims testable. Whether a seat beside the entrance feels welcoming or exposed takes observation and judgement.

In a design studio you read a brief, draw alternatives and take them to a critique. A tutor might ask you to trace one person’s journey or defend the least attractive seat in the room. A floor plan is a view from above. Sections show height, while models and material studies help investigate questions a plan leaves open. This commission concentrates on layout, circulation and explaining a choice.

The commission

Refit one 8 m by 6 m reading room using its existing furniture. Deliver a plan that seats twelve, keeps the returns desk reachable and gives the librarian a reason to favour one arrangement. First diagnose the room, then draw two alternatives. File one before you open the afternoon observation. Your last submission is an annotated drawing and a short handover.

A calculator is optional. All measurements and rules are supplied. Work through the site survey in one sitting and return for the studio if you prefer. Browser saving and export are available throughout.

Walk the room before moving anything

Follow one adult from the entrance to the returns desk, then follow a child joining the reading club.

The people behind the brief

Librarian: “Keep the route along the south side. I need to see arrivals from the desk.” Club volunteer: “The six children read one picture book together. I want their chairs around the same table.” Regular reader: “I come for the window seat. I can hear the door from half the room.”

North is at the top. The entrance opens in the west wall onto the shaded 2 m strip. Follow the strip east to the west face of the desk. Fixed shelves occupy the north-east wall. The window runs from 0.6 m to 4.6 m along the north wall. Each grid square is 0.2 m by 0.2 m. Read dimensions from the grid when the screen changes size.

Measured survey

The tinted footprint includes the table, pulled-out chairs and their operating space. The six club chairs occupy a 2.4 × 2.0 m kit. Each of three quiet pairs occupies 1.6 × 1.6 m. Solid rectangles are tables and the small squares are occupied chairs. The drawing shows the empty queue condition.

A scale of 1:50 means that 1 cm on a paper drawing represents 50 cm in the room. For example, a 3 m wall would measure 6 cm.

Describe the adult’s route and the child’s route. Name where those journeys could conflict and which user you would observe to check your interpretation.

Who gets the north window?

Use the same four pieces twice. Your second drawing must serve a different priority.

Studio brief

In A put the club at the window. In B move the club away and give at least one quiet pair that place. A table counts as being at the window when its footprint starts within 1 m of the north wall and at least half its width overlaps the window span. Annotate at least one piece in each drawing.

Retain all twelve seats. Keep occupied footprints inside the walls and clear of each other and the fixed fittings. For each chair a 0.6 m square must be able to travel from the entrance to the cell behind its back. Inside the shaded southern strip, a 1.2 m square must travel continuously to the desk. Both squares move horizontally or vertically. Touching edges is allowed. These dimensions are the librarian’s rules for this exercise.

The route readout gives the largest square that can make the whole journey. A turn can limit the route even where individual gaps look wide enough. A dotted centreline shows one passing path when a path exists.

The librarian’s observation

At 4 pm six children are seated at the club table. The other six seats are occupied too. Four adults wait along the south wall, starting by the desk and extending west. Each occupies a 0.6 m square. The desk and the queue positions are fixed for this test. Place the four visitors using the queue control. Watch where the route moves as the line grows.

On the original survey the club’s south edge is at 4.8 m from the north wall. At their closest point the visitors’ north edges are at 5.4 m. Use the difference to diagnose the original pinch point, then check your own drawing.
