An introduction to economics and finance

Begin with the discipline, then examine the context of your investigation.

What is economics?

Economics examines how people, organisations and societies allocate limited resources, and how those choices affect others. It investigates questions ranging from how prices influence behaviour to why unemployment persists or living standards differ.

Microeconomics focuses on individuals, firms and markets. Macroeconomics examines economies as a whole, including growth, inflation and unemployment. Both require attention to assumptions and evidence.

Where finance connects

Finance studies how money is raised, allocated and managed over time. It considers investment and funding decisions under uncertainty, including whether an organisation can meet its commitments when they fall due. In this Lab, financial analysis helps establish whether an economically attractive proposal can actually be delivered.

How these disciplines are studied

Studying economics and finance involves constructing models, interpreting data and assessing arguments. A model simplifies a situation so that a particular relationship can be examined; its conclusions depend on the assumptions used. Mathematics and statistics support this work, alongside careful reading and analytical writing.

In this introductory case, you will work with supplied forecasts and a defined set of constraints. Your task is to establish what the evidence supports, explain the implications and identify an assumption that would need verification in practice.

Your investigation

Evaluating the BrightBridge workshop

BrightBridge is a fictional community learning organisation considering a Saturday workshop. Its organiser needs a recommendation on whether the proposal can meet its attendance and financial objectives. You will analyse demand forecasts, supplier quotations and a payment schedule, then reassess the proposal when its conditions change.

Your final output: a financial comparison and a reasoned recommendation specifying whether the workshop should proceed, the appropriate ticket price and the funding arrangement required.

What the terms mean

Demand = students who want a place at a given price.
Actual attendance = students who get a seat and attend.
Revenue = ticket income, before costs.
Surplus = event income left after event costs.
Cash balance = money available at a particular time.

See a worked example with different numbers

Imagine a different event: 40 attendees, a ₹250 ticket, ₹100 variable cost per person, ₹3,000 fixed costs and ₹1,000 sponsorship.

  1. Ticket revenue: 40 × ₹250 = ₹10,000.
  2. Variable costs: 40 × ₹100 = ₹4,000.
  3. Total costs: ₹3,000 + ₹4,000 = ₹7,000.
  4. Surplus: ₹10,000 + ₹1,000 − ₹7,000 = ₹4,000.

The bank balance could still be low before the tickets are paid. That is why you will build a separate cash schedule.

The scope of your investigation

Economics: how do prices, limited seats and the organiser’s goals affect choices? Finance: can the plan fund its costs when they fall due? As you work, notice whether you enjoy interpreting the situation, checking calculations or explaining a recommendation.

This case samples applied microeconomics and financial planning. Economics also includes inflation, unemployment, inequality, policy and statistical research; finance also includes investment, valuation and risk. You will see the wider study map at the end.

Attendance = smaller of demand and capacity.

Contribution and break-even

For this part, use a ₹500 ticket and the original ₹180 cost per person. Contribution is the amount from one ticket left after its variable cost. It helps cover fixed costs. Break-even means a surplus of at least zero; it is different from the organiser’s ₹20,000 target.

Help with break-even and rounding

With sponsorship: minimum attendees = (fixed costs − sponsorship) ÷ contribution, rounded up to the next whole person. Without sponsorship: fixed costs ÷ contribution, rounded up. For example, 12.2 attendees means you need 13, not 12.

Step 4 of 6 · One connected case

Evaluate the advice against the evidence

Assess each statement using the case facts and analytical notes below. Explain the basis for your judgement.

Evidence C · Three analyst notes

Note A

Surplus and cash measure different things. Event surplus compares all event income and costs. A cash schedule records when money enters and leaves. Loan principal creates a repayment obligation; it is not event income. A fee for borrowing or delayed payment is a cost.

Note B

A result depends on its assumptions. Here, forecasts are treated as exact so your answer can be checked. In practice, demand would need evidence. A lower-price, higher-demand forecast by itself does not prove that changing price causes the whole difference. Other influences might change too.

Note C

Financial cost is not every economic cost. Volunteer time can have an opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative forgone. It need not appear as a cash payment. A capacity limit also means that interest in an event is not the same as a completed sale.

These are original Edussentials learning notes. For a broader treatment of opportunity cost, see OpenStax, section 2.1 after completing the case.

Supplier’s optional offer

Move exactly ₹3,000 of the materials bill from the before-event payment to Event day. Pay that ₹3,000 plus a ₹300 fee immediately after the final ticket receipt, before sponsorship arrives. The fee is an additional event cost. The ₹3,000 is a delayed payment of an existing cost.

Your decision rule: choose the standard schedule if it meets attendance ≥120, surplus ≥₹20,000 and loan ≤₹15,000. Otherwise choose the supplier offer if it meets all three. If neither does, do not proceed under these terms. No other price or funding source is available.

Help with a deferred payment

Moving an existing bill to a later date reduces the earlier cash gap. It does not erase the bill or increase surplus. A new fee is different: it is an extra cost. Check the later stage too, because paying the deferred bill can create another cash low point.

Further study in economics and finance

This investigation introduced a particular form of applied analysis: assessing a proposal within stated constraints and revising a recommendation when the evidence changes. Economics and finance extend far beyond this setting. The sources below offer a starting point for examining that wider academic landscape.

Economics at university

Expect microeconomics, macroeconomics, mathematics and statistics, followed by empirical methods such as econometrics. You use graphs, equations, data and written arguments to investigate choices and outcomes. One concrete example is LSE’s BSc Economics curriculum; programmes differ.

Finance at university

You move from cash and accounts to time value of money, investment appraisal and risk. The question becomes not only “Can we pay?” but “Is this use of money worthwhile, given timing and uncertainty?” OpenStax’s time-value section offers a first glimpse.
