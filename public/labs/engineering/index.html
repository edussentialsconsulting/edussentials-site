The East Wall garden

Every morning a volunteer has 45 minutes to water twelve beds. The committee wants to know what to buy and what evidence to ask for before spending its money.

What engineers study

Engineering brings mathematics, physical science and design together to make things work under constraints. Civil engineers might plan how water moves across a site, mechanical engineers examine how a pump performs, and electrical engineers consider the energy it draws. Here you will work at the point where those decisions meet.

At university, a fluid mechanics calculation might predict a flow rate. A laboratory class asks you to measure it. In a design studio, you have to explain a discrepancy and decide what it changes for the people using the system. You will do a small version of each in this case.

Your commission

Prepare a purchasing note a volunteer committee can act on. You will mark out a route, compare suppliers, draw a system in words, and specify a trial. The field log opens after you file your first proposal. Keep a calculator handy. Ratios, multiplication and division are all you need. The figures are fictional and all the evidence is here.

Work in two sittings if you prefer. A natural stopping point is the saved proposal. At the end, export your work to keep both your original design and your final advice.

Read the site as an engineer

The volunteer’s account and the site plan describe different parts of the same problem.

From the garden coordinator

“We start with 520 litres of usable rainwater in the tank. Each of our twelve beds needs 40 litres at its inlet every morning. We water one bed at a time. The footpath is the only entrance and must stay clear. Our daily pump allowance is 95 watt-hours. The equipment budget is ₹10,000. Among systems that meet every limit, buy the least expensive.”

Survey sketch · plan view

The 45-minute limit applies to pumping time. Allow zero switching time and zero leakage in this model. Use the total litres delivered at the beds. Energy and price limits are inclusive. A design must pass all limits.

Identify one requirement a faster pump cannot solve. Explain how a volunteer could check that every bed received its share even if the total volume was correct.

Read the units

Flow is volume delivered per minute. Running time in minutes = required litres ÷ flow. Energy in watt-hours = input watts × running minutes ÷ 60. Price is paid once. Energy is consumed on every run.

For example, delivering 150 L at 10 L/min takes 15 minutes. A 100 W pump operating for those 15 minutes consumes 25 Wh. A powerful pump can finish quickly and still use more energy over the run.

Enter exact numbers without units. Compare each result with the coordinator’s limits.

Write this page as if someone else will have to run your trial tomorrow.

The purchasing conversation

The treasurer favours the lowest purchase price. A volunteer favours a quicker finish because the gate closes on time. Use the coordinator’s buying rule to nominate a kit for a trial. Record how much time the quicker feasible kit would save and what it would cost to gain that time.

Use arrows or a labelled text sketch. Show the source, pump, permitted hose route and metered outlets. Mark where you would measure delivered flow and how you would stop each bed at its required volume.

Name the kit you would trial first. Use time, energy and purchase price to support it. Give the strongest case for a different feasible kit and identify one assumption that could reverse your decision.

Specify the route, where to collect water, what to measure and in which units. Explain one condition to hold constant, why to repeat the test, and what result would send your design back for revision.

Write the purchasing note

The committee needs a decision today and a practical acceptance test before it signs off.

In two short paragraphs, recommend the exact kit and hose. Quantify its margins against time, energy and budget. Compare the next best option. Set out what the volunteer should measure on the first full run and what result would make you pause the purchase. Explain how a 35-minute slot would change your advice.

Review your note

Could the treasurer identify exactly what to order? Could a volunteer tell when to stop watering a bed? Can both see where measurement replaced an assumption? Your calculations receive automatic feedback. Use these questions to review the quality of your advice.

Recall a point where calculation, drawing, testing or explaining a compromise changed your thinking. Which part would you choose to investigate in a college lab, and why?
